mLearn

I started studying Japanese seriously in February 2024. By July, I'd made [a href="https://github.com/adrianvla/intelligent-subtitles"]Intelligent Subtitles[/a], a tool to help me learn from Japanese videos without constantly interrupting what I was watching.

Then, in October 2024, I was bored in informatics class. I had my computer with me, so I decided to package Intelligent Subtitles into an all-in-one application that I could keep extending. That was the first version of mLearn.

A month later, I used it to watch Hunter × Hunter entirely in Japanese, with Japanese subtitles. It was the first time I'd watched something that way, and it made a tremendous difference to my Japanese. Around a year after I'd started studying, I could sustain everyday conversations in the language.

That experience is still the main idea behind mLearn: learning through things you actually want to watch and read, rather than constantly stepping away from them to study.

The application has grown considerably since that first version, with several rewrites and a rather long list of additions. I originally built it for myself, but other people use it now too.

Current versions and downloads are at [a href="https://mlearn.kikan.net"]mlearn.kikan.net[/a]. The software is source-available; [a href="https://github.com/adrianvla/mlearn"]the repository[/a] is on GitHub.

Watching & reading

The original problem was simple: pausing a video, looking up a word, losing track of the sentence, and doing it all again gets exhausting surprisingly quickly.

mLearn makes subtitles interactive. You can click a word to see its reading and definitions, then continue watching. There's also a subtitle overlay for other applications. The reader supports text documents and manga, including OCR for images and pages where the text can't be selected.

Japanese makes this more complicated than it initially sounds. A written form can have multiple readings and meanings, prosody patterns, and even deciding where one word ends and another begins isn't always trivial. Making dictionary lookups useful in context requires tokenization, dictionaries, and disambiguation rather than simply replacing each word with a translation.

Remembering words

Looking up a word is one thing. Remembering it is another.

mLearn tracks what you've encountered and what you've explicitly reviewed. Review cards can retain the original sentence, audio, and a screenshot/video clip, so you can return to the context in which you first encountered a word. I've also experimented with probes for checking uncertain vocabulary, and there's Anki integration for people who prefer their existing cards (like me who has about 12k tracked notes in it).

The word-knowledge model has probably taken a disproportionate amount of my time. Seeing a word once, recognizing it in a sentence, and being able to recall it independently are three very different things. Treating all three as simply "known" or "unknown" loses quite a lot of information.

The rest of it

Over time I've added OCR, configurable vocabulary displays, pitch-accent and reading support, video tooling, and local or cloud model options for explanations and speech workflows. Some of these began as small experiments; others required rather substantial changes to the application.

The exact feature set varies between releases, so the current website is the best place for up-to-date details.
